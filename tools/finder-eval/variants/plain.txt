The 12 fallacies, each with its pattern in quotes:
Straw Man: Twists what someone said into a wilder claim, then attacks that. "You said A, so you must want extreme B."
Ad Hominem: Rejects an idea by insulting the person's looks, background, group, or character. "Ignore him, he is a loser."
False Dilemma: Offers only two choices when more exist. "Either A or B, nothing else."
Appeal to Ignorance: Treats missing proof as proof. "Nobody has disproved it, so it is true."
Slippery Slope: Predicts one small step will snowball into disaster. "If we allow A, then B, then C, then disaster."
Circular Reasoning: The reason just repeats the claim in other words. "It is good because it is good."
Hasty Generalization: Turns one or two cases into a rule about everyone. "I saw it once, so they are all like that."
Appeal to Authority: The only reason is who said it. "A famous or expert person said it, so it is true."
Red Herring: Dodges the point by changing the subject. "Never mind that, what about this other thing?"
Equivocation: One word quietly switches meaning midway. "A is B, B is C, so A is C" while the word shifts.
Appeal to Emotion: Pushes fear, guilt, pity, or pride instead of a reason. "Feel this, so do that."
Tu Quoque: Blocks criticism by saying the critic does it too. "You do it too, so you cannot criticise me."

How to tell look-alikes apart:
- Slippery Slope predicts what will HAPPEN next (soon, then, eventually, end up, before you know it). Straw Man twists what someone WANTS.
- Tu Quoque only when the speaker is answering criticism with "you do it too". Any other insult or put-down is Ad Hominem.
- Appeal to Authority names an outside person or source (influencer, celebrity, coach, trainer, expert, endorser). Circular Reasoning names nobody; the claim vouches for itself.
- Red Herring includes "Yes, but look at..." and answering a question with an unrelated topic.
- Appeal to Emotion includes poor, innocent, cruel, how could you, imagine your kids, guilt-trips, and scary slogans with no chain of events.
- Hasty Generalization includes sweeping claims about all, every, none, always, never, or a whole group, backed by one story or nothing.
